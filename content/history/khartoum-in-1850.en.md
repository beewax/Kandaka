In late December 1850 an English family rode out of the Bayuda Desert and crossed to the meeting of the two Niles. Their son, George Melly, was twenty. He kept a journal, and the next year he published it in London as *Khartoum and the Blue and White Niles*. His chapters on the town give one of the few close, dated descriptions of Khartoum in the middle of the Turco-Egyptian period, thirty-five years before the Mahdist siege.

## A town of mud and gardens

Seen from the river, Melly writes, Khartoum was "a long wall of mud," with a few buildings showing above it: the governor's offices, the old government house, and the Catholic church and mission. He counted about **3,000 houses**, almost all built of sun-dried mud. There were no broad streets, only an occasional open square. Yet the better houses stood in gardens of orange, lime, banana and pomegranate, and his family's lodging looked over the Blue Nile from a high bank. He admits that with such gardens and the winter climate, "it is not difficult to be content to live between walls of mud."

## People and faiths

Melly gives the population as about **30,000, including the garrison**. These are a traveller's figures, not a census. Muslims were the great majority. The Christians numbered about **fifty**, almost all connected with the Roman Catholic mission, which had three priests, a church and a small school. There were about **twelve Jews**. He describes the three communities living together mostly in peace, with occasional outbreaks of intolerance, and notes that all were taxed with little favouritism.

## Markets and trade

The market, he writes, had **four covered streets and four open ones**. The covered streets held the best shops, stocked with Manchester printed cottons, Sheffield knives and scissors, and Staffordshire pottery. The open streets were mostly stalls of senna, lichens, herbs and dried plants. Khartoum's merchants sent out **gum arabic, gall-nuts, senna, castor oil and large quantities of ivory**. The goods went down the Nile to Berber, then by camel across the desert to Korosko in Egypt. Boat-building was busy along the river, mostly long open boats of palm wood.

## The governor and the "honourable exiles"

The family was received by the governor-general, **Abd al-Latif Pasha** (in office 1850–1852), whom Melly found generous: he gave them a house, lent them his own boat, and promised thirty camels to meet them at Berber. Melly also noticed how many senior officials were in Khartoum against their will. He calls it an "honourable exile." The governor of Berber, Ali Bey Hasib, was one of them. So was **Bayoumi Effendi**, a mathematician trained at the École Polytechnique in Paris, who had been ordered to found a school in Khartoum with twelve teachers. Melly found no school building and no pupils, and judged the whole scheme a pretext to remove able men from Cairo. Rifa'a al-Tahtawi, sent to Khartoum in 1850, appears in the same chapter under his title "Rifa'a Bey."

## Rain and daily life

The worst season, Melly was told, was the rains. They came so suddenly and so heavily that a man visiting a friend might be stuck in his house for three or four days until the water went down. He also records everyday beliefs, such as avoiding the river on the last Wednesday of the year until after the afternoon prayer.

## Reading Melly today

Melly wrote as a young Englishman of his time. His book contains condescending judgments about the people he met, and harsh remarks about religion. The new Arabic translation keeps his text complete and adds short notes at those passages, along with notes that explain names, places and measurements. Read that way, his Khartoum chapters are a valuable record of a city that would be besieged, destroyed and rebuilt within a lifetime.
