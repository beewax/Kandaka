## A mineral is not yet an industry

Sudan should take its mineral possibilities seriously. It should take claims about their economic value even more seriously. Between identifying a mineral occurrence and establishing a viable industry lies a sequence of questions about evidence, costs, institutions, environmental protection and public benefit. Skipping those questions does not accelerate development. It makes development harder to judge.

Uranium is especially vulnerable to exaggerated expectations because it brings together three powerful ideas: underground wealth, electricity and national technological ambition. Yet these are different subjects. Evidence that uranium occurs in a country does not establish a commercially recoverable reserve. A prospective mine does not establish that a domestic nuclear power programme is affordable. Neither proposition establishes that the resulting revenues would reach citizens.

The appropriate starting point is therefore not a prediction of a uranium boom. It is a public standard of proof. Sudan should be able to investigate its resources without being required either to promise an imminent windfall or to abandon investigation altogether.

This distinction matters when identifying historical material rather than presenting it as new research.

The presentation combines general geology and mineral-occurrence material with a substantial illustration of gold mining and processing, followed by uranium-related slides. The gold-mine photographs do not demonstrate an operating uranium industry. Nor does a concession map by itself establish a commercially recoverable uranium reserve. It is a useful historical starting point, not a current investment appraisal.

One identifiable research trail is Adam and Eltayeb's study of 30 surface phosphate samples from Kurun and Uro, 15 from each area. It reports higher average uranium content in Uro. Its journal issue is labelled 2009, while the paper records receipt and final-form dates in 2010. Its conclusions and tables also contain inconsistent range reporting, so this review does not reproduce a headline concentration. [Original journal paper, pp. 166–177](https://www.aqa.org.ar/images/anales/pdf9702/9702art13.pdf).

Sampling establishes observations about the material examined. It does not, on its own, establish deposit-wide tonnage, economically recoverable quantities or an investment return. A modern assessment would need independent validation and explicit limits on how far the evidence can be generalized. No reserve estimate or profitability claim is endorsed here.

## Three questions, not one headline

The United Nations Framework Classification separates environmental-socio-economic viability, technical feasibility and confidence in quantities. Those dimensions offer a useful discipline: confidence that material exists is not equivalent to confidence that a project is viable. The framework is a classification system, not certification that any particular Sudanese project meets its criteria. [UNECE, UNFC 2019](https://unece.org/sites/default/files/2023-10/UNFC_ES61_Update_2019.pdf).

Kandaka's proposed test for public discussion is correspondingly simple. What has actually been demonstrated? What would have to be demonstrated next? Who would independently check the result? A statement that cannot answer these questions should remain a possibility rather than enter a national revenue forecast.

A credible public record should distinguish an old reconnaissance observation from a recent independently reviewed estimate. It should identify the responsible institution, the assessment date, the classification used and the unresolved limitations. Historical findings should not acquire the appearance of new discoveries merely because they circulate again online.

This discipline also protects competent Sudanese scientists. Researchers should not have to defend political promises that exceed their findings. Geological uncertainty is an ordinary feature of exploration, not a professional failure. A project that does not proceed can still leave useful scientific knowledge, provided that the research and its limitations are preserved honestly.

## Public value must be designed before a contract

If further evidence justified commercial investigation, the next question would be how Sudan would evaluate competing proposals. The objective should be public value over the life of a project, not the largest announced investment number.

Kandaka proposes independent scrutiny of ownership, financing assumptions, public liabilities, environmental obligations and the distribution of benefits. A government should understand what it is committing before offering guarantees, tax concessions or associated infrastructure. An investor's optimistic estimate should not become the state's budgeting assumption.

Local participation should also mean more than the location of the deposit. Communities should receive understandable information, a meaningful opportunity to question proposals, and accessible ways to raise grievances. Consultation should account for people displaced from an area, seasonal land users and households whose rights are poorly documented. The absence of a person during conflict must not be treated as the absence of an interest.

Employment and training commitments should be specific enough to audit. So should environmental monitoring and the financing of closure and long-term obligations. Promises that depend entirely on future goodwill are weak protections, particularly when public institutions themselves need rebuilding.

These are proposed standards, not claims that Sudan currently has such arrangements in place. Establishing whether existing institutions could enforce them would be part of any serious readiness assessment.

## Uranium policy and electricity policy need separate decisions

A national electricity strategy should start with the services people and firms need: reliable clinics, water supply, communications, household access and productive activity. It should compare feasible options on their costs, delivery risks, reliability and institutional demands. A mineral discovery should not predetermine that comparison.

The [IAEA Milestones approach](https://nucleus.iaea.org/sites/nids/capacity/milestones/SitePages/Home.aspx) treats nuclear power as a substantial institutional undertaking. Its infrastructure issues include safety, regulation, financing, the electrical grid, human resources, safeguards, security and radioactive waste management. Possession of uranium does not resolve those requirements.

Kandaka therefore recommends separating three decisions: whether to improve geological knowledge; whether a particular mineral project merits further evaluation; and whether nuclear power belongs in a future electricity system. Each requires its own evidence and public justification. Approval of one should not be presented as approval of the others.

That separation avoids a false choice between technological ambition and immediate needs. Sudan can strengthen scientific education and public regulatory competence while choosing near-term electricity investments through an independent service-delivery assessment. Long-term options need not be confused with present commitments.

## Examine the wider phosphate question

Kandaka proposes evaluating the phosphate question as a connected development problem: could a properly assessed project contribute to agriculture or industry, and could any associated mineral recovery improve its public value? Neither answer should be assumed from a laboratory finding. The appraisal would need to consider product quality, infrastructure, water, energy, workforce capability, waste obligations and the market for the main product as well as any by-product.

Value addition should not become a slogan that excuses an uneconomic plant. Compare realistic alternatives, including postponement, and test who bears the downside if prices, financing or operating conditions disappoint. Local ownership and skills development matter, but a project must also justify the public resources it consumes.

Communities in the Nuba Mountains should not appear in such a discussion only as locations on a geological map. Kandaka's proposed development test includes regional benefit, meaningful participation by women and marginalized groups, protection of livelihoods, and transparent treatment of land and displacement. An attractive national revenue story cannot substitute for those obligations.

## A practical first programme

The first phase should be modest enough to verify. Assemble existing records, reconcile their identities, assess their quality and publish a readable account of what is known and unknown. This would include a clear distinction between documented evidence and claims requiring confirmation. Publication of sensitive personal or security information would need appropriate safeguards.

A second phase, if justified, should commission independent professional assessment under transparent terms. The questions and decision criteria should be agreed before results arrive. Otherwise every finding can be interpreted to support a predetermined announcement.

A third phase would consider specific development proposals only if the preceding evidence justified doing so. The public should see why a proposal advances, pauses or stops. No revenue projection should be presented without its uncertainty and assumptions.

Success in the first year should consequently be measured by better records, competent oversight and clearer decisions, not by an arbitrary number of licences or a headline valuation. An informed decision not to pursue a weak project is better than an expensive commitment made to preserve appearances.

Sudan's mineral future deserves ambition disciplined by evidence. Uranium might warrant further investigation; the evidence must determine how far. The most valuable immediate resource is not a number repeated in a headline, but the capacity to distinguish a possibility from a project that genuinely serves the country.
