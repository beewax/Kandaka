**Robert O. Collins · Cambridge University Press · Available in the Kandaka Library**

Collins's *A History of Modern Sudan* is the standard English-language reference for Sudan's political history from the Turco-Egyptian conquest of 1820 through the late post-independence period. It is the work that contextualises every economic analysis, every development plan failure, and every institutional crisis discussed elsewhere in the Kandaka library. You cannot read the World Bank's 2015 Country Economic Memorandum, or the Industrial Location Analysis, or any analysis of the Gezira Scheme, without the political and institutional history this book provides.

## The Arc of the Argument

Collins structures Sudan's modern history around a recurring pattern: the extraction of Sudanese resources and labour by successive external powers, the failure of each governing arrangement to build inclusive institutions, and the recurrence of peripheral rebellions against a Nile valley centre that has never found a stable way to share power or wealth with the country's enormous hinterland.

The Turco-Egyptian period (1820–1885) established the extractive template: Sudan was conquered for gold and slave soldiers. Finding less gold than expected and encountering sustained resistance to slave recruitment, the administration fell back on cotton and taxation. The brutal tax regime triggered the 1822 Shendi revolt and established the pattern of peripheral resistance to central extraction that has characterised every subsequent governing arrangement.

The Mahdiyya (1885–1898) — the Islamic revolutionary state that expelled the Turco-Egyptians — was both a genuine popular uprising against colonial extraction and a fragile attempt to build an Islamic state on Sudan's terms. Collins's treatment of this period is essential reading alongside the Formation of the Sudanese Mahdist State paper in the library, which analyses the ceremonial and symbolic construction of Mahdist authority.

The Anglo-Egyptian Condominium (1898–1956) restructured Sudan's economy around cotton export for British mills, deliberately prevented industrial development, and built administrative institutions designed to manage a colonial economy rather than develop a self-sustaining one. The legacy of this period — geographic inequality, cotton dependency, underdeveloped institutional capacity — is the direct inheritance of post-independence Sudan.

## Why This Book Matters for Development Analysis

The question development economists consistently fail to ask about Sudan is: why have institutions been so persistently extractive? Collins provides the historical answer. Each governing power — Turco-Egyptian, Mahdist, Anglo-Egyptian, post-independence civilian, military — has faced the same structural challenge: a geographically enormous, ecologically diverse, ethnically complex country in which the centre has insufficient legitimacy, capacity, and revenue to govern the periphery without coercion. Each has resolved this challenge by extracting from the periphery to fund the centre, producing the peripheral rebellions that eventually consume the governing arrangement.

Understanding this history does not make the problem easier to solve. But it makes the solution clearer: any development strategy that does not fundamentally address the centre-periphery extraction dynamic — through genuine fiscal decentralisation, cooperative ownership of productive assets, and inclusive institutions that distribute rather than concentrate economic power — will reproduce the same pattern.
