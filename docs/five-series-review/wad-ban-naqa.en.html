Wad Ban Naqa: A Royal City Beyond the Familiar Pyramids

DRAFT FOR OWNER REVIEW — NOT APPROVED FOR PUBLICATION
Initial manuscript; research and image checks remain. مسودة أولى؛ البحث وفحص الصور لم يكتملَا.

A photograph of a pyramid announces itself. A spread of brick walls asks more of the viewer. We have to imagine the difference between an entrance and a blocked passage, a room and its later reuse, a fragment in a museum and the building from which it came. At Wad Ban Naqa, those relationships are the beginning of the story.

The site belongs to the world of Meroitic Kush, but it is not simply another stop in a procession of pyramids. Its palace, temples and excavated deposits offer ways to understand royal authority, religious activity and the changing use of a place. The interesting question is not how closely it resembles a better-known monument. It is what its own evidence allows us to see.

First, put the right place on the map

Wad Ban Naqa is not the same site as Naqa. Similar names, varied transliterations and images circulating without careful captions can blur the distinction. A photograph of a temple at Naqa should not become an illustration of Wad Ban Naqa merely because both belong to ancient Kush.

The National Museum in Prague’s introductory publications situate Wad Ban Naqa in a history of Sudanese and international investigation. Sudan’s Antiquities Service excavated there in 1958–1960; the museum’s expedition began in 2009 in cooperation with Sudanese antiquities authorities. The later work should be described as part of that history, not as a discovery of a place previously unknown.[1][2]

That matters beyond courtesy. Earlier excavation records determine which parts of a building were exposed, altered or interpreted before another team arrived. Understanding the modern history of the site helps us judge the ancient history proposed for it.

A palace is evidence, not a complete biography

The building known as the Palace of Queen Amanishakheto is central to the site’s interpretation. The museum’s dedicated fifth report treats the palace through architectural study, excavation and its wider setting.[3] A popular article can explain why such a building matters without turning its conventional identification into a detailed account of a ruler’s daily life.

Rooms and passages can suggest how access was organized. Construction and storage can raise questions about labour and resources. But a ground plan alone cannot name every occupant or tell us what happened on an ordinary afternoon. The temptation to fill a royal building with invented conversations makes the past vivid at the cost of making it less reliable.

The more rewarding approach is to show readers the surviving clues and explain the reasoning. A rebuilt wall is not automatically part of the first phase. An object found nearby does not necessarily date the whole structure. Archaeological interpretation becomes easier to follow when those relationships are made visible.

Temples that changed

The preliminary report on the twenty-first excavation season describes work in the so-called Isis Temple and a nearby chapel. Researchers examined parts of the temple’s circulation and sanctuary spaces, recovered decorated and inscribed fragments, and identified later burials over the temple. The report also records a discard area associated with cult equipment and goods.[4]

Together, those observations point to a place with a history rather than a single frozen moment. Religious buildings were constructed and used; material was broken, deposited or displaced; later people used the ground differently. A visitor looking only for an intact facade can miss that sequence.

This does not mean every object has an obvious explanation. A deposit may tell us what was thrown away more readily than why. Establishing the order of events requires the position of finds, the layers around them and comparison with other dated material. A handsome isolated object is only part of the evidence.

Following an object beyond Sudan

A sandstone barque stand from Wad Ban Naqa is now recorded in Berlin under inventory number ÄM 7261. The proposed illustration shows the object in a museum, photographed in 2023—not the archaeological site as it looks today.[5] That distinction should be explicit wherever the picture appears.

An object’s present display and its ancient function belong to different histories. Its decoration can be studied alongside excavated architecture and early records, but the journey into a European collection also deserves attention. Provenance is not a footnote that begins only when an object reaches a showcase.

For this article, the photograph is an invitation to look closely at an actual surviving work, not a substitute for a reconstructed temple scene. Any reconstruction would need to distinguish measured remains from conjecture. A persuasive illustration can make an uncertain arrangement seem settled more easily than a paragraph can.

What preservation should make possible

Wad Ban Naqa deserves a public explanation that begins with its evidence and the people responsible for studying and protecting it. That means readable Arabic interpretation as well as specialist publications, careful distinctions between original fabric and modern conservation, and photographs dated clearly enough not to suggest present conditions they cannot establish.

It also means respecting the site as more than a collection of objects suitable for removal or reproduction. Walls, deposits and relationships between buildings carry information that a museum photograph cannot preserve by itself. Protecting those relationships is part of protecting the story.

The familiar pyramid gives Sudan a powerful public image. Wad Ban Naqa expands that image. It asks us to see royal and religious life through architecture, fragments, changing ground and the work of interpretation. The result can be more compelling than a perfect imaginary city: a place whose history becomes clearer as we learn how to look.
