Does Sudan Need a Ministry of Innovation—or Better Support for Inventors?
DRAFT FOR OWNER REVIEW — NOT APPROVED FOR PUBLICATION

An improved pump is useful when someone can build it consistently, repair it and sell it to people who need it. Between a clever prototype and that ordinary achievement lie testing, finance, components, standards, customers and practical experience. A minister can announce support for innovation without making any of those steps easier.

That does not make public institutions irrelevant. It makes their design important. Sudan’s question should be which institution will solve which problem, with what authority and how its performance will be checked. A new ministry is one possible administrative choice. It is not a substitute for answering those questions.

Start with capability

The World Bank’s Innovation Paradox argues that developing countries often lack the complementary capabilities needed to benefit from technology, including management and organizational skills. Its policy argument extends beyond research expenditure to the ability of firms to adopt and use knowledge. This is a comparative framework, not a tested prescription for Sudan.[1]

Its useful challenge is simple: a country can import an advanced machine without acquiring the ability to use it well. It can fund research without connecting a result to a viable product. Conversely, an improvement in maintenance or production organization can be valuable without generating a spectacular patent.

The workshop, the laboratory and the customer therefore belong in the same conversation. Repair workers may know why equipment fails under local conditions. Researchers may be able to test a proposed improvement. Producers need to discover whether customers value it enough to pay its full cost. None of those participants should be reduced to a ceremonial guest at an innovation conference.

What Sudan must preserve

In October 2026 UNESCO described an assessment of Sudan’s science and innovation system and a proposed recovery framework. It emphasized scientific infrastructure, researchers, institutional capacity and connections between scientists. The framework is an institutional proposal; its announcement does not establish that the envisaged funding has been secured or that the work has delivered results.[2]

The practical implication is that expertise deserves attention alongside buildings. Reopening a laboratory without technicians, consumables, calibration and a maintenance budget can leave an impressive room with little usable capacity. A displaced researcher’s continued connection to colleagues can matter even before physical return becomes possible.

Our existing universities article examines institutions that serve society. This feature asks a more specific question: how can someone outside a university obtain the testing, technical advice or partnership needed to improve a product?[3] The answer should include small firms and regional workshops, not only businesses with established connections in the capital.

Three functions before one new signboard

Kandaka proposes testing the administrative options against three functions. First, access to practical services: testing, technical advice, shared equipment and help understanding standards. Second, transparent finance for clearly defined development work. Third, opportunities to prove a product through properly governed trials and procurement.

A new ministry might coordinate those functions if it has a clear mandate, capable staff and effective relationships with existing institutions. An agency might deliver some of them with less administrative duplication. Existing bodies might be able to perform them if their responsibilities and budgets are repaired. The choice requires a map of current mandates and bottlenecks, not an assumption that a new name creates a new capability.

The test is what changes for the applicant. Can a small producer find out where to take a sample, what the test costs and when a result will arrive? Can an unsuccessful applicant understand why a proposal was rejected? Can a supplier demonstrate a product without needing a political sponsor?

Support that learns rather than protects forever

Public support can give a promising activity time to develop. It can also preserve an unsuccessful project when the people administering it cannot admit failure. The distinction lies partly in the terms set before funding begins.

A pilot should name the practical uncertainty it will test. A grant to improve a storage container should be judged against relevant performance, durability and cost requirements—not simply whether a prototype was displayed. Subsequent support should depend on evidence. Closing an unsuccessful pilot can be a useful result if the findings are honestly recorded and available to others.

Competition should be real, but identical application demands can disadvantage smaller applicants. Proportionate requirements, published criteria and technical assistance could widen participation without abandoning scrutiny. Intellectual-property arrangements should be understood before a collaboration begins, including the contributions and interests of researchers, workers and public funders.

Count useful change

Patent statistics offer only one view of inventive activity. The WIPO country profile contains missing observations; a blank cell must not be interpreted as proof that Sudanese people do not innovate.[4] Nor would a larger patent count alone demonstrate that locally useful products became available.

Better questions concern reliable services delivered, technical problems solved, products that meet standards, firms able to maintain equipment and learning shared beyond the original beneficiary. The results will not all appear quickly. That makes honest intermediate measures important, rather than an excuse to measure only announcements.

Sudan may ultimately decide that a ministry is the right vehicle. It should arrive at that decision through the work that needs doing. A new office earns its place when it makes a difficult practical step easier for people with useful ideas. The country needs that service whether the sign above the door says innovation or something else.
