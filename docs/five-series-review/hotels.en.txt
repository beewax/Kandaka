Sudan’s Historic Hotels: Buildings, Visitors and Changing Cities
DRAFT FOR OWNER REVIEW — NOT APPROVED FOR PUBLICATION

A hotel keeps two kinds of history. One is visible: its facade, rooms, staircases and alterations. The other exists in work and memory: who prepared the meals, arranged a journey, maintained a building, greeted a returning guest or translated a difficult conversation. A history that records only famous visitors preserves the first names in the guest book and loses much of the institution.

Sudan’s hotels deserve attention as places where a city met people arriving from elsewhere. They belonged to commerce, travel, family enterprise and changing ideas of comfort. Their history can be told without turning it into a list of grand buildings or a claim that every old establishment enjoyed a golden age.

The evidence is uneven. This opening account concentrates on Khartoum, where accessible records and photographs are stronger. It should not stand in for the whole country. Port Sudan, Atbara and other towns need their own documented hotel histories rather than a passing mention added to make the map appear complete.

The Acropole as an institution

In a personal scholarly obituary, archaeologist Alexandros Tsakos traces the Acropole’s founding to Panaghis Pagoulatos in 1952 and describes the family’s subsequent role in running it. He recalls meeting George Pagoulatos in 1994 and being introduced to other archaeologists. His account explains how hospitality and practical knowledge could help fieldwork begin.[1]

That is valuable testimony from a participant, not a complete business archive. It tells us what the hotel meant to a particular community. It should be read alongside other accounts and the work of the Sudanese staff whose contribution cannot be reconstructed simply from the prominence of the owners’ names.

The useful idea is that a hotel can provide more than accommodation. A person arriving in an unfamiliar place needs reliable advice, transport arrangements, introductions and help understanding procedures. Such services depend on experience and trust. They are difficult to see in a photograph of a bedroom and difficult to replace by constructing a newer building.

The building and the people are different archives

A photograph may establish the appearance of a facade on a particular date. It cannot by itself establish a founding date, uninterrupted ownership or the quality of the service. Promotional descriptions often combine those claims as though they were a single fact.

The Grand Hotel’s Commons contributor describes the establishment as dating to 1902. That date needs independent documentary confirmation before being presented as settled history. The proposed courtyard photograph is useful for a more modest purpose: it shows an earlier architectural setting. Its capture date remains unconfirmed, despite a camera timestamp that should not be trusted automatically.[2]

This distinction allows photographs to remain enjoyable without asking them to prove what they cannot. We can look at balconies, shade and circulation, and ask how a building accommodated heat and sociability. We should not infer the experiences of every guest or worker from a carefully selected view.

Whose hospitality becomes history?

Hotels employ people whose knowledge is rarely commemorated in plaques. The cook who knows what can be obtained reliably, the maintenance worker who keeps water available and the receptionist who remembers a complicated booking help create continuity. A serious hotel history should seek their names and accounts, where consent and evidence allow.

It should also ask who could afford to enter, who was welcome and which forms of service reflected the inequalities of their time. Recognizing those questions does not require stripping a place of affection. It makes affection more honest. A building can be beloved while its history contains unequal relationships.

Travellers’ memoirs are useful but situated. A visitor may remember a hotel as the centre of a city because it was the centre of their own stay. Residents experienced a much larger city. Comparing guest accounts with local newspapers, advertisements, employment histories and family records can prevent one familiar viewpoint from becoming the whole story.

Memory after damage

NPR’s June 2025 report included testimony from the Pagoulatos family about the Acropole and its destruction during the war. It offers a dated account of loss, not a current inspection of every part of the property.[3] Neither that report nor an older photograph should be used to imply that a hotel is operating or safe to visit now.

But the story need not end at destruction. Preserving a menu, a dated photograph, an architectural drawing or a worker’s recorded recollection can preserve evidence that would otherwise disappear. Families and institutions should retain originals, document permission and identify what is known about each item. An uncertain date is better recorded as uncertain than converted into a confident caption.

Rebuilding, if it becomes possible, would raise choices about what to retain and whom the revived institution should serve. A historic appearance alone cannot recreate the relationships that made a place distinctive. Nor does every alteration erase heritage. The task is to understand what has value before deciding what should change.

A wider Sudanese story

The most promising history of Sudanese hotels would connect buildings with routes and livelihoods: why travellers came to a town, how they reached it, who hosted them and what services grew around them. Hotels would then become a way of reading urban change, rather than decorative backgrounds to someone else’s adventure.

That is also their relevance to a development website. Hospitality draws together construction, food, transport, languages, maintenance and human judgment. Its history shows that service economies are made of skills and relationships as well as beds. Remembering those connections gives Sudan more to work with than a catalogue of lost facades.
