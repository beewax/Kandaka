One monument can no longer be visited. The other still rises twelve metres above the desert.

Karanog was a Kushite town in Lower Nubia, on the west bank of the Nile near Aniba and Qasr Ibrim, in what is now southern Egypt. Its cemetery yielded nearly eight hundred excavated graves, Meroitic inscriptions, painted pottery, carved stone figures, textiles, glass, bronze and woodwork. The landscape in which it stood disappeared beneath Lake Nasser after the construction of the Aswan High Dam.

Farther south, in Sudan, the building traditionally known as the Throne Hall of Dongola still dominates the ruins of Old Dongola. Built in the ninth century, probably as a royal audience hall for the kings of Makuria, it became a mosque in 1317 and remained a place of worship into the late 1960s. Its walls preserve the marks of both histories.

At first these may seem like unrelated places: one a cemetery of the later Meroitic period, the other a royal building of medieval Christian Nubia; one known largely through objects removed during an early twentieth-century excavation, the other still standing where its builders placed it. Yet together they correct one of the most persistent habits in writing Nubian history. Nubia is too often presented as the southern margin of somebody else's world—Egyptian, Roman, Byzantine, Arab or Ottoman. Karanog and Dongola show something more interesting. Nubians borrowed, traded, translated and rebuilt, but they did so inside societies with their own rulers, languages, religious lives and ideas of authority.

## Karanog Was Not a Roman Cemetery

The title of the original excavation report, published in 1910 by C. Leonard Woolley and David Randall-MacIver, was *Karanog: The Romano-Nubian Cemetery*. The phrase still appears in catalogues, and it is useful only if handled carefully. The excavators themselves presented “Romano-Nubian” as a deliberately non-committal label for Lower Nubia during the period of Roman rule in Egypt. It does not mean that Karanog was a Roman colony or that its inhabitants were culturally Roman.

The cemetery belonged to the northern world of Meroitic Kush. Its people wrote in Meroitic, buried their dead according to local practices and used objects whose forms and meanings drew on several traditions at once. Roman glass and Mediterranean bronze vessels reached Karanog through exchange; Egyptian religious forms were reshaped; distinctively Nubian painted pottery carried animals, plants and human scenes in a confident local visual language. The imported object did not erase the society that selected and used it.

Modern scholarship has also moved beyond the excavators' confident identification of the people of Karanog as Blemmyes. That identity was not written by the people buried there: the report constructed it from classical accounts of Rome's southern frontier and from racial typologies, including supposed distinctions in skull and facial form. The excavation remains indispensable because it recorded a vast body of material, but its ethnic labels and evolutionary judgments belong to an early colonial archaeology that too readily interpreted African creativity as borrowed and African change as decline.

Chronology requires the same care. The 1910 report proposed a sequence extending from around the beginning of the Common Era into the fifth century, with the third century best represented. Modern typological work has revised that framework; Karanog is better understood as a major Meroitic settlement and elite cemetery whose richest late phases illuminate especially the second and third centuries CE, while the full sequence remains open to reassessment.

The site was excavated between 1907 and 1910 by the University of Pennsylvania's Eckley B. Coxe Junior Expedition. The cemetery lay a little north of Aniba; the associated fortified settlement stood at Karanog itself. The excavators recorded nearly eight hundred graves, containing between one and eleven burials each, and estimated that the cemetery held 3,000–4,000 interments; only 1,124 sets of remains were individually counted in their summary, so the larger figure should be read as an estimate rather than a population census. The plan records a crowded landscape rather than a row of isolated graves. A minority of tombs had small mud-brick or masonry superstructures, offering places and approaches above underground chambers. Some tombs were reused, some disturbed, and many belonged to families or communities whose stories cannot be reduced to the most spectacular find.

## The Dead Were Remembered in Public

Karanog's best-known sculptures are the ba statues—sandstone human figures that the excavators associated with the Egyptian idea of the *ba*, one aspect of a person's continuing existence after death. The name is convenient, but the figures are not copies of an Egyptian formula. At Karanog, standing men and women wear local clothing and jewellery, hold ritual objects and sometimes carry a disc above the head. Their recorded findspots include the front of a tomb approach and the ground around a superstructure. They appear to have given the deceased a visible presence among the living.

The reconstruction is a historical document of early twentieth-century interpretation, not an untouched record of the statue's original appearance. Woolley and Randall-MacIver, &lt;em&gt;Karanog&lt;/em&gt;, plate 1. Public domain; digitised by the Institute of Fine Arts, New York University.&lt;/p&gt;

That public presence matters. A cemetery is not only a place in which bodies were deposited. It is an architecture of memory. The carved figure, offering table, inscribed stela and decorated tomb approach created a point of encounter between the dead, their families and visitors. Meroitic writing made names and formulas visible even though the language is still only partly understood today. Food and drink offerings made remembrance an action, not simply an idea.

The grave goods broaden the picture. Fine painted pottery shares space with glass imported from Roman Egypt, bronze bowls, iron weapons and tools, beads, cosmetics, leatherwork, textiles and inlaid wooden caskets. These were not random treasures. They expressed gender, office, wealth, family standing, ritual expectation and access to trade. An inscribed sandstone offering table now at the Penn Museum, for example, combines Meroitic text with carved bread and libation vessels. It is at once an object of language, food, ritual and status.

The pottery is perhaps the clearest rebuttal to the idea of cultural dependence. Some vessel shapes or motifs travelled over long distances, but the combinations are unmistakably local. Giraffes stand beside stylised plants; cattle and herders move around bowls; geometric bands organise surfaces with remarkable control. These objects were technically accomplished, visually playful and socially meaningful. They show artisans making choices, not passively receiving influence.

## What Was Lost When the Water Rose

Karanog also forces a difficult question about how knowledge is preserved. The excavation saved plans, photographs, drawings and objects before the region was transformed by the dams at Aswan. Without that work, much would be unknown. Yet archaeological rescue was also a process of removal. The report states that the Egyptian government retained a representative selection of painted pottery and bronzes, including the bowl from tomb G187, together with most inscribed stones; the remainder—including most painted vessels, beads, rings, caskets and many bronzes—went to the University Museum in Philadelphia. The excavators also acknowledged that superstructures often had to be destroyed before they could open the chambers below. Objects survived, but the cemetery was emptied, parts of its architecture were sacrificed, and the publications filtered the people of Karanog through the categories of their excavators.

The right response is neither to dismiss the archive nor to treat it as neutral. The plates can be read twice: first for what they document, and again for how they frame it. A restored statue may reveal surviving colour while also revealing the restorer's assumptions. A plan may preserve the position of hundreds of graves while reducing generations of mourning to numbered shapes. Museum catalogues can reconnect objects to tomb groups, but only if provenance remains central and digital access does not substitute for the claims of source communities.

Karanog is therefore both an ancient site and a modern lesson. Physical preservation came too late for the landscape. Intellectual preservation remains unfinished because old collections can still be restudied with better chronologies, scientific analysis and questions that take Meroitic society on its own terms.

## Dongola's Building of Many Lives

Old Dongola presents the opposite visual experience. The visitor does not begin with a tray of objects or a century-old plan. The building is there—massive, battered and visible from a distance on its rocky outcrop at the edge of the medieval city.

Old Dongola, known in Old Nubian as Tungul, emerged near the end of the fifth century and became the capital of Makuria, one of medieval Africa's most durable kingdoms. Makuria adopted Christianity in the sixth century, developed its own ecclesiastical and artistic traditions, wrote in Old Nubian as well as Greek and Coptic, and maintained relations with both Byzantine and Islamic worlds. Its survival cannot be explained as isolation. It depended on diplomacy, military power, agriculture, trade and the ability to translate foreign forms into Nubian institutions.

Photograph by LeGabrie, 2017, via Wikimedia Commons, &lt;a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/"&gt;CC BY-SA 4.0&lt;/a&gt;; no changes.&lt;/p&gt;

The building is about 28 metres long, 18 metres wide and 12 metres high. Massive mud-brick walls, reinforced and faced in places with fired red brick and sandstone, enclose two floors. On the ground level, tall narrow rooms were roofed with barrel vaults. A monumental western staircase led official visitors upward, away from the domestic scale of the town and toward the upper ceremonial rooms and roof terrace.

The identification as a throne hall is persuasive, but it is still an archaeological interpretation. Scholars have considered a church, monastery or royal castle. What favours an official audience function is the combination of the unusually high lower floor, the processional stair, the commanding upper storey and the arrangement of the principal room. The central hall, nearly square at about seven metres on each side, was surrounded by a corridor. Four granite supports carried a wooden coffered ceiling, probably rising over the centre. A deep apse opened from the eastern side. The architecture directed movement and controlled sight: one approached, ascended, circulated and finally entered the place where authority appeared.

The wall paintings gave this authority a Christian language. Surviving fragments include royal figures, warrior saints, archangels and complex scenes centred on Christ and the Cross. One composition placed Christ and the four living creatures of Revelation within a cross above rows of white-robed elders. Another fragment preserves a royal crown. The programme joined kingship to a sacred cosmos, but it did so through Nubian painting, Nubian patrons and a hall in a Nubian capital. Byzantine courtly ideas were resources to be adapted, not proof that Dongola was governed from Constantinople.

## 1317 Was a Transformation, Not an Erasure

In 1317 the upper hall was converted into a mosque. A foundation inscription records the change during a period of Mamluk intervention and dynastic struggle, when Abdallah Barshambu was installed as king. A mihrab was cut into the east wall, a minbar was added, openings were altered, the older paintings were covered with plaster, and the building entered a new religious and civic life.

It is tempting to turn that date into a simple ending: Christian Nubia disappeared and Islamic Sudan began. The archaeology refuses such a clean line. Churches continued to function in Dongola after the conversion. Christian and Muslim communities, institutions and memories overlapped. The royal court did not leave the city until 1364, and even then Dongola continued as the centre of a smaller kingdom. Islamisation was a long social process, not a switch thrown in one room on one day.

The conversion also preserved as it transformed. Covering the Christian paintings hid them, but in some places the plaster protected fragile colour for later conservators. The building's continued use demanded repairs and alterations that obscured its ninth-century form while keeping the structure alive for more than six centuries. By the time it ceased to function as a mosque in the late 1960s, it had served Muslim worshippers far longer than it had served Makurian kings.

Calling it only the “Throne Hall” therefore hides most of its biography; calling it only the “Mosque Building” hides the royal phase that shaped it. The double name is not a problem to solve. It is evidence of the way Sudanese monuments accumulate communities rather than belonging permanently to one.

## Two Monuments Against the Margins

Karanog and Dongola should not be collapsed into a story of unbroken sameness. They were separated by geography, religion, language and at least seven centuries. Karanog's elite buried their dead within the later Kingdom of Kush; Dongola's rulers governed a Christian kingdom that arose long after Meroitic political power had ended. Historical continuity is not a bloodline drawn across every interruption.

The connection is instead a repeated political and cultural practice. At Karanog, artisans and patrons selected Egyptian religious forms and Mediterranean goods, then placed them inside Meroitic burial customs and local systems of status. At Dongola, architects and painters selected forms associated with churches and imperial audience halls, then made them serve Makurian kingship. In 1317, another community altered the same royal building for Islamic worship without reducing it to rubble. In every case, reception was active.

This is why the two monuments matter to Sudanese and Nubian history today. They shift attention from the question “Who influenced Nubia?” to better questions: Who chose what to borrow? What did they change? Which institutions gave a foreign form a local meaning? Who maintained it, and who was later written out of its story?

They also offer two warnings about preservation. Karanog shows the finality of a landscape submerged and a community dispersed through museum collections. Dongola shows that survival in place is not safety: mud-brick architecture needs continuous conservation, local stewardship and political stability. High-resolution scans, open catalogues, three-dimensional records and original reconstructions can widen access, but they cannot replace the monument, the landscape or the authority of the people whose heritage it is.

Nubia was never merely a corridor between more important civilisations. Karanog's cemetery and Dongola's many-lived hall were centres in their own worlds. Their makers looked north and south, across deserts and along the Nile, and turned what travelled into something recognisably their own. That is not peripheral history. It is how historical cultures are made.
